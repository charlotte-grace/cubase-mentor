# Music Theory for Production

Working theory for melodies, chords, bass, and rhythm. Genre-free, practical over
correct — the goal is making choices on purpose, not passing an exam. Teach one
section at a time. Builds on the theory floor in fundamentals.md.

## Scales as palettes

A key is a palette of seven allowed notes; stay inside it and nothing clashes hard.
Natural minor is the everyday default. Two relationships worth knowing:

- **Relative major/minor** share the exact same notes (A minor = C major) — moving
  between them changes mood without changing palette. The smoothest "key change"
  there is.
- **Neighbouring keys** (a fifth up or down) differ by one note — section changes
  into a neighbour feel related but fresh. Moving everything up a semitone is the
  classic energy lift: maximally unrelated, maximally noticeable. Use once, late,
  if at all.

**Key choice has a physical constraint.** The sub-bass fundamental lands on the
key's root — and roots around E1–G1 (~41–49 Hz) reproduce on most playback systems
while lower roots often don't (see fundamentals: low end). When the key is a free
choice, keys whose root sits in that zone (E, F, F#, G and their minors) give the
low end a head start. Transposing a finished idea a tone up or down to move the sub
into the zone is a legitimate, cheap fix.

Modes recolour the same seven notes by choosing a different home. Two useful ones:
Dorian (minor with a brighter 6th — hopeful minor) and Phrygian (minor with a
flattened 2nd — dark, tense, one semitone above home always looming). Try a bassline
on the same root in each; the flavour difference is immediate.

## Melody: which notes work when

- **Strong beats want chord tones** (notes of the chord playing underneath);
  the notes between chord tones work as passing notes on weak beats. A "wrong"
  note on a weak beat is spice; on a strong beat it's a mistake — unless resolved
  immediately down or up one step, which turns it into the best trick in melody.
- **The pentatonic subset** (scale minus its two clash-prone notes; in minor: drop
  the 2nd and 6th) is the safety palette — almost any pentatonic note works over
  almost any diatonic chord. Start pentatonic, add the spicier notes back
  deliberately.
- **Intervals have characters:** octaves and fifths are stable and open; thirds and
  sixths are sweet; seconds and sevenths rub; the tritone is maximum tension.
  Melody against bass makes intervals constantly — sweetness and rub are choices.
- **Phrasing beats note choice.** Call and response: two bars that ask, two that
  answer. Repetition with one change per phrase. Leave gaps — the silence between
  phrases is what makes them phrases.

## Building chords

- **Triad = root + third + fifth.** The third decides everything: minor third = sad,
  major third = bright. That one semitone is most of harmony.
- **Extensions add sophistication cheaply:** the 7th makes a triad wistful; the 9th
  makes it lush; **sus2 and sus4** delete the third entirely — neither major nor
  minor, pure suspension, endlessly useful for ambiguous, atmospheric harmony.
- **Diatonic chords:** build a chord on each note of the scale using only scale
  notes and you get the key's chord palette (in minor, the workhorses: i, III, iv,
  v, VI, VII). Pick from the palette and you cannot leave the key by accident.
- **Progression recipes to start from** (roman numerals, minor key): i–VI–III–VII
  (the four-chord engine), i–iv (dark and minimal), i–VII–VI (descending pull),
  and the two-chord home↔away loop from the fundamentals. Steal one, then change
  one chord.
- **Voicing outranks progression.** Inversions let the next chord reuse notes the
  current one already holds — good progressions move as few fingers as possible
  (voice leading). Spread a chord wide for a lush pad; cluster it tight in the mids
  for a stab; drop the chord's root entirely and let the bass carry it — the chord
  gets lighter and the low end gets cleaner.

## Bass variation

Root notes following the chords is correct and boring. Variation, in order of risk:

1. **Rhythm before pitch** — most bassline variation is rhythmic: same note, new
   subdivision pattern. This is where basslines live or die.
2. **Octave jumps** — same note, new register; instant movement, zero harmonic risk.
3. **The fifth** — the safe second pitch over any chord.
4. **Approach notes** — slide into the next root from a semitone or tone below,
   landing ON the chord change. The approach is tension, the landing is release.
5. **Vary the last bar** — keep bars 1–3 of a 4-bar loop identical, change bar 4:
   the loop breathes without losing its identity.

Bass and kick share the floor: decide who owns which hits (interlock), or duck the
bass under the kick (see fundamentals: carving space in time). A bassline written
against the kick pattern, in the gaps, grooves before any processing touches it.

## Syncopation

Syncopation is emphasis where the grid doesn't expect it — off-beats, anticipations
(hitting just before the beat, "the push"), and rests where hits were promised.

- It only reads against something straight: keep one element (usually the kick)
  anchored, syncopate against it. Syncopate everything and the groove dissolves —
  tension needs a ruler to push against.
- **Off-beat bass** (notes on the "and"s between kick hits) is the fastest route to
  bounce; **anticipation** (the note arriving a 16th early, tied over the barline)
  is the fastest route to urgency.
- **Deletion is a syncopation tool:** write the part straight, then remove the most
  expected hit. The ear leans into the gap; the gap is the hook.
- Work in 16th-note offsets first — smaller than that is groove/swing territory
  (see fundamentals), larger changes the pattern itself.

## How to use this file

Never teach it top to bottom. Pull the one section the current musical problem
needs, connect it to the sound the user is actually making, and send them back to
the DAW with one thing to try.
